Nike EHQ · Amsterdam (NL)

Designing Nike EMEA's new campaign execution operating model (and securing $25M quarterly revenue)

Built the processes, timelines, and communication framework that delivered four consecutive seasons with 100% on-time launches and ensuring $25M quarterly revenue.

Context

Nike EMEA ran its seasonal campaigns across 200+ stores with no standardised coordination process spanning the full execution cycle. Merchandising, Assortment Planning, Retail Marketing, Allocation, and VM&S teams operated on their own timelines with their own deliverables. Product teams built sequentially and handed off downstream. Marketing ran a parallel track, while VM&S and Allocation were informed late in the cycle. The two tracks were not intersecting enough before launch.

I joined as one of the early members of a newly formed execution team created specifically to address this gap, with a mandate to design, improve and own the coordination model for Men's Sportswear, Nike's biggest category representing roughly $25M in quarterly demand.

Problem

The absence of a shared cadence meant teams were making decisions in isolation. By the time misalignments surfaced, they were already expensive. Stores received campaign marketing without the product to support it because assortment shifts happened after marketing had already been briefed. Late-stage corrections triggered costly last-minute reassortment and drove avoidable sell-through losses. Nobody was failing individually. The system itself had no mechanism for catching cross-functional drift before it became a store problem.

What I built

Organizational Mapping & Stakeholder Analysis
  • Mapped every function's role, interdependencies, deliverables and decision points across the full seasonal cycle.

Understand the system before redesigning it.

New Operating Rhythm
  • Introduced a new biweekly meeting involving operational stakeholders.
  • Deliberately kept leadership team out so execution conversations could happen without escalation pressure.

Bring together all teams on a structured cadence and create a shared visibility layer that had not previously existed to proactively address issues.

Enhanced Communication
  • Set up a dedicated Slack channel.

Surface work-in-progress in real time rather than waiting for final deliverables.

Knowledge Management
  • Centralized all key documents in a single shared folder with clean version control, acting as the central POC and owner.
  • Built a master timeline in Airtable tracking all workstreams end-to-end.
  • Created an analytics file that compared deliverable inputs across teams, flagging discrepancies before they became launch problems.

Give every stakeholder a single source of truth.

The structural act was not any single tool. It was redesigning how information flowed across the system, and getting 20+ stakeholders to operate inside a shared accountability framework.

Results

  • 100% on-time delivery across all key products and campaigns over four consecutive seasons.
  • Every delay in product delivery and every change in campaign visuals was caught and compensated before launch, saving $M.

The late-stage misalignments that had previously been routine stopped occurring, because the process was designed to surface drift weeks before it could reach stores.

What I learned

  • Cross-functional coordination failures stem from the absence of a shared operating model and the lack of visibility across teams.

What was missing was a designed system for information to flow across them in time to act on it. Building that system was as much an information architecture problem as it was a process design problem, and the most important design decision was making it structurally safe for execution-level conversations to happen without triggering leadership escalation.

Foxintelligence · Paris (FR)

Saving the FMCG vertical (and €2M ARR) by reintegrating missing e-retailer's sales data

Context

Leclerc is the leading French online grocery retailer, accounting for roughly half the FMCG market by value. Until early 2021, their confirmation emails contained no transaction detail, no amount, no product, no category. Foxintelligence could count orders placed on leclerc.fr, but could say nothing about what they were worth. For FMCG brands buying market intelligence, that gap was disqualifying. You cannot sell a market view that is missing half the market.

Problem

In early 2021, Leclerc reinstated transaction details in confirmation emails. That fixed the forward-looking problem and immediately created a new one: the moment Leclerc data was reintegrated, their value market share jumped from 0% to roughly 50% overnight. Every competitor's share was mechanically halved, Carrefour dropped from 30% to 15%, showing an apparent month-on-month decline of 50%.

None of that was real. It was a data artifact, and it made every trend analysis covering the transition period worthless. And we had no historical Leclerc transaction data to work with and needed to reconstruct what we had never seen.

What I built

With and Without Leclerc filter
  • Implemented new filter in the platform to fully add/remove Leclerc from scope of analysis preventing trend analyses from being distorted during the transition period.

Buy time with a temporary fix while the permanent solution is built.

Backward Propagation Model using BigQuery
  • Sorted every historical Leclerc order (without details) chronologically by week.
  • Ranked each one within its week using order datetime (1 to N).
  • Built a cumulative percentile index, assigning each order its relative position in the weekly distribution (1/N to N/N).
  • Using post-2021 Leclerc data, computed a stable weekly category split and average order value per category (e.g., 30% of orders mapped to fresh produce at an AOV of €28, 15% to vegetables at an AOV of €13).
  • Each historical order was then matched to its percentile bucket and assigned the corresponding category and expected value (e.g., the first ]0,30%] orders assigned to "vegetables", the next ]30,45%] to fruits, etc.)

Use newly observed Leclerc data to retroactively infer value and category attribution for all historical orders and reconstruct all historical data.

Results

  • Statistically defensible reconstruction of every Leclerc order pre-2021.
  • Signing of new clients (Super U, Intermarché, Auchan, Monoprix), contributing to €2M in incremental ARR.
  • Replicable framework extended to merchants omitting transaction details from confirmation emails (incl. Amazon).
  • Boost in client confidence in the team's technical ability to resolve unanticipated data challenges.

What I learned

  • Proactivity and transparency with clients is key.
  • Quick fix first, permanent solution second.
  • If you intuitively believe the solution to a problem exists, then it most certainly does.

Clients sometimes do not need perfect data: they need a sound method honestly presented and the confidence to explain its assumptions transparently.

Nike EHQ · Amsterdam (NL)

Fixing Nike's campaign readout process (and driving +20% sales growth the following year)

Context

Cross-functional campaign readouts at Nike were fragmented by design. Each analytics team defined their own questions, ran their analysis independently, and contributed slides to a shared deck that regularly topped 100, with inconsistent formatting, disconnected findings, and no unifying narrative. Leadership walked away with volume, not clarity.

Nobody had questioned it. It was simply how things were done.

Problem

The Cyberweek readout, Nike's most commercially significant campaign moment of the year, was being run the same way: insights were not building on each other, but were competing for airtime. Decisions that should have been grounded in a coherent commercial story were being made against a fragmented one.

I volunteered to redesign the process from scratch rather than contribute to another isolated workstream.

What I built

Pre-analysis alignment
  • Consolidated and rationalized the learning agenda by identifying overlaps across teams and bucketing related questions into single encapsulating ones.
  • Held cross-functional leadership accountable for defining how insights would actually drive decisions (not just what questions were interesting).
  • Aligned all analytics teams on a shared learning agenda before a single query was run.
  • Secured final LT sign-off.

Full visibility and agreement on who answers each question and what output the business expects.

Unified project structure
  • Clear owners per section.
  • Common analytical perimeter: shared date ranges and platform scope.
  • Single deck skeleton worked from simultaneously, with a fixed slide allocation per question.

Make collaboration structurally impossible to avoid.

New Operating Rhythm
  • Daily check-ins to surface new findings, unblock data dependencies, and flag conflicts in real time.

Teams built on each other's work throughout the process rather than discovering it at the final readout when it was too late to integrate.

Results

  • Output went from 100 slides down to 10: a single coherent narrative that leadership could act on.
  • Fewer insights, but significantly better received: curated, connected, and decision-ready.
  • Insights directly informed the following year's Cyberweek planning, contributing to a verified +20% incremental sales growth.
  • The process became the new standard for campaign readouts across all teams.

Less becomes more: fewer slides for sharper decisions and lasting process change.

What I learned

  • Define what success looks like before the first query is run, not after the deck is built.
  • Fixing the output meant fixing the process upstream, before the analysis started, not after.

The failure was never analytical quality. Teams were producing rigorous work in isolation, but failed to reason about how the business would actually use an abundance of sometimes conflicting insights.

Nike EHQ · Amsterdam (NL)

Building a new members' retention framework for Nike (that lifted member repeat purchase by +3pp)

Context

Nike's EMEA membership programme had a structural retention problem. 70% of members had never made a purchase, and of those who had, 58% had only bought once. The business knew the numbers but had no systematic way to understand why some first-time buyers came back while others did not, let alone which levers to pull to change it.

Even the definition of retention wasn't properly aligned across the company.

Problem

The core analytical challenge was scope: defining a member journey across every product category, platform, demographics, time of purchase etc… would have produced billions of journey permutations.

The problem had to be scoped deliberately before it could be solved rigorously.

What I built

Which journey to go after
  • Defined what a journey is: a first conversion purchase in a given product category on a given platform. That's it.
  • From there, prioritised the 15 highest-volume purchase journeys across EMEA digital, beginning with Men's NSW Footwear buyers on Nike.com.
Methodology
  • Segmented first-time buyers into two cohorts: those who returned within 18 months and those who did not.
  • Normalized the comparison window to 18 months post-purchase to ensure the groups were comparable regardless of when the initial purchase happened.
  • Analyzed discriminant factors with business input, comparing share of repeat buyers per factor.

Some analyses showed positive signals:

Some others remained inconclusive:

Tableau dashboard for long-term vision
  • Designed a cohort-style Tableau dashboard that let business teams explore activation-to-retention funnels for any of the 15 journeys without analytical support.
  • Teams could select their journey, their (pre-defined) analysis, and see % change between 1-time buyers and repeat buyers.

Make the insights actionable beyond the analytics team.

Results

  • For the selected journey, identified levers informed a series of A/B tests that lifted repeat purchase rate +4pp (e.g., starting with a promo, buying an Air Max instead of an Air Force, migrating to the Nike App within the first 90 days).
  • Scaled across the programme, the framework contributed an estimated 3pp increase in member retention company-wide.
  • Cross-functional teams were empowered to self-serve and find insights for their own journey relative to their scope.
  • Project was awarded best Insights and Analytics project across FY23, chosen from a cohort of 80 peers.
  • Framework later informed retention strategy for priority performance categories.

Turn a retention diagnosis into a scalable, self-serve system with measurable business impact.

What I learned

  • The most valuable design decision was scoping the problem to 15 journeys and going after the most valuable one, rather than attempting to model everything.
  • Building something a non-analyst could use was what made the framework durable.

Tools that require an analyst to interpret them get used once. Tools that empower teams to ask their own questions get embedded.

Le Wagon · Paris (FR)

Building a CNN model for AI-generated image detection (reaching 92.7% accuracy)

See on GitHub

📋 About this Project

This team project was made in the context of Le Wagon's Data Science & AI bootcamp (March 2026), aiming to build a deep learning model deployed end-to-end to tackle a real-world problem.

Through this project we built a binary classifier that distinguishes between real images and synthetic images created by AI generators like DALL-E, Midjourney, or Stable Diffusion.

🎯 Our project revolved around 3 main challenges:

  • Data preprocessing: Loading and cleaning tens of thousands of images stored on Google Cloud Storage, detecting corrupted files, and organizing them into train/validation/test splits.
  • Model development: Building and training multiple architectures to learn the subtle patterns that differentiate real from AI-generated images.
  • Production pipeline: Building a complete workflow from raw images to fully trained and deployed model, with weights/training checkpointing and cloud storage integration.

🌱 Environment and Tools

  • Programming Language: Python 3.10.6
  • Versioning: Git / GitHub
  • DL Framework: TensorFlow
  • API Framework: FastAPI
  • Containerization: Docker
  • Cloud Platform: Google Cloud Platform

📊 Dataset Used

Our models were trained using a dataset of 60,000 images from the AI-generated-images-vs-real-images dataset on Kaggle.

  • 30,000 AI generated images
    • 10,000 from MidJourney
    • 10,000 DALL-E
    • 10,000 Stable Diffusion
  • 30,000 Real images
    • 22,500 from Pexels and Unsplash
    • 7,500 from WikiArt

🔎 Methodological Approach

1 — Data Preprocessing

The images from the dataset had vastly different sizes and resolutions. We opted for offline preprocessing with 2 techniques: a resize to 256×256 pixels, and a random crop to 256×256 pixels. We settled on resize since it performed slightly better and avoided cases where the crop could randomly land in a location less prone to show AI generation patterns.

2 — Model Selection
  • Baseline CNN (3 hidden convolutional layers)
  • Custom Complex CNN (10 hidden convolutional layers)
  • VGG16
  • EfficientNetB4
  • ResNet50
3 — Evaluation Metric

We chose accuracy as the primary metric since we wanted to minimise both False Positives and False Negatives equally.

4 — Results
  • Baseline CNN: 80.5% accuracy
  • EfficientNetB4: 87.2% accuracy
  • ResNet50: 89.1% accuracy (92.7% with fine tuning)

Foxintelligence · Paris (FR)

Launching Foxintelligence's freemium model (and growing new user acquisition by 40%)

Context

Initially, Foxintelligence's only routes to new customers were paid subscriptions and sales-led live demos, meaning top-of-funnel velocity was capped by the capacity of the sales team, and leadership wanted to increase organic inbound and reduce reliance on outbound.

As Product Lead, I was tasked with designing and shipping a freemium tier: an open, self-serve version of the platform that would attract prospects and drive conversion without requiring a sales touchpoint to get started.

Problem

The freemium experience had to surface enough value to create genuine product desire, while withholding enough to make the paid tier worth converting to.

Get it wrong in either direction and the model fails.

What I built

On the product side: defined the freemium experience
  • Which analyses and filters were accessible.
  • What data scope was available.
  • What remained locked to surface the value of the paid tier without giving it away.
On the conversion side: designed a lead capture flow
  • Collecting name, company, function, and email at signup.
  • Added a request-a-demo CTA to route warm leads directly into the sales pipeline.
On the data side: worked with engineering to build new routes
  • Real-time pipelines and Slack alerts notifying sales reps the moment a prospect company signed up.
  • Automatic CRM updates triggered.

The full model followed a classic Acquisition → Activation → Conversion funnel logic.

Results

  • +40% new signups growth within the first three months of launch.
  • +25% sales-qualified leads.
  • 10% freemium-to-paid conversion rate.

What I learned

Freemium is as much a sales tool as a product decision, and the most important design choices were about information architecture and lead routing.

Nike EHQ · Amsterdam (NL)

Rebuilding Nike's analytics intake process (and increasing team's project delivery rate +20%)

Context

Joining Nike's Digital Commerce Analytics team, requests came from everywhere: Slack, email, direct messages, hallway conversations, with no filter, structure or shared understanding of capacity.

The team was constantly over-committed, under-delivering, and slowly losing credibility.

Problem

There was no structured cadence for qualifying incoming requests, no shared visibility on who was working on what, and no defensible mechanism for prioritization. The team could not say no, not because the work was unimportant, but because there was no framework to make the decision legible to anyone outside the team.

The goal was to build a process that let the team honour its commitments, and decline requests without it feeling personal.

What I built

Quarterly intake process aligned with Nike's seasonal cadence
  • Teams logged projects the quarter before.
  • Collective leadership review three weeks before the start of each quarter to prioritize the full slate.
  • Projects dispatched based on analyst skills, project complexity, and individual preference.
  • 20% buffer reserved for genuine ad-hoc requests.
Pre-quarter scoping with each stakeholder
  • Timelines, deliverables, and shareout dates defined before the quarter began.
  • Once locked, the perimeter moved only for rare, leadership-validated exceptions.
Transparency as the keystone
  • Every project in flight visible to any stakeholder at any time.
  • Workload needed no justification, it was on display.
  • Bypass requests discussed against already-aligned and visible priorities. No arbitrariness, no perception of bad faith.
Managing out-of-process requests
  • Buffer absorbed quick asks while full analyses waited for the next quarter, subject to leadership-validated priority.
  • Strict one-in-one-out logic for genuine emergencies. New project replaced an existing one or consumed the analyst's buffer entirely.
  • Team capacity was full. That logic was non-negotiable, and the process made it defensible to any stakeholder.

Results

  • +20% team project delivery rate, owing to better resource allocation and significantly less dispersion.
  • Trust restored, with stakeholders knowing what to expect, and when.
  • Saying no became structural rather than personal: the process carried the decision, so no individual had to.

What I learned

Capacity problems are rarely capacity problems. They are visibility problems. The team was not under-resourced — it was under-structured. Once every stakeholder could see the full picture, the dynamic shifted: requests became negotiations rather than demands, and the team's workload became a shared constraint rather than a black box to push against.

What made this possible: a pattern learned at Foxintelligence

The intake process at Nike was not built from scratch. It was grounded in a dynamic I had already navigated as Data Product Lead at Foxintelligence, where sales teams regularly pushed features requested by their prospects during sprint prioritization, bypassing the roadmap and pressuring the product team directly.

At Foxintelligence, every request was evaluated against three questions:

1 — Company's strategic direction
  • Where does the company want to head towards?
2 — Feature value
  • How much revenue would this feature generate, and is it replicable for other clients?
3 — Efforts needed
  • Is this feature an easy quick-win or a weeks-long product improvement?

Each was scored via RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) so the decision was transparent and collective. The framework decided which to prioritize, not the product manager or the sales lead.

Critically, the team maintained a public Notion ticket board with outward weekly communication on current priorities and the reasoning behind them. Stakeholders did not need to ask what was being worked on or why because the visibility was accessible to all.

The same principle translated directly to Nike: make capacity visible, make prioritization legible, and the conversation shifts from push-and-resist to shared constraint.
